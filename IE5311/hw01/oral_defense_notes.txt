IE 5311 HOMEWORK 01 - ORAL DEFENCE NOTES
Not part of the submission. Four topics flagged for questioning.

Rule for all four: answer the mechanism, not the result. The numbers are
in the write-up; what gets probed is whether you know why they came out
that way, and where each claim stops being true.


================================================================
1. LAZY CONSTRAINT CALLBACKS
================================================================

THE ONE-SENTENCE ANSWER
Both methods add subtour constraints on demand; the difference is that
constraint generation throws away the branch-and-bound tree after every
round and lazy constraints keep one tree for the whole solve.

THE MECHANISM, IF PRESSED
  - Gurobi fires the callback at the MIPSOL event, meaning it has found
    an integer-feasible incumbent.
  - You call cbGetSolution to read that incumbent, run your separation
    routine on it (here: find simple cycles in the selected arcs), and
    if any constraint is violated you pass it back with cbLazy.
  - The solver rejects that incumbent and carries on from the same node,
    keeping its bounds, pseudo-costs and already-proven prunings.
  - You must set model.Params.LazyConstraints = 1 BEFORE optimize(),
    because it disables presolve reductions that would otherwise be
    unsafe. Presolve cannot reason about constraints that do not exist
    yet.

THE TRAP, AND THE HONEST ANSWER
Q: "So lazy constraints are faster?"
A: Not uniformly, and our own data says so. Aggregate ratio 53.6 to 1,
   but instance 3 alone is 98% of the total CG time. Median ratio 2.5 to
   1. On instances 1 and 10 constraint generation was FASTER, because
   those needed one to three rounds and the callback overhead was never
   repaid. The correct claim is conditional: the saving scales with the
   number of separation rounds that would otherwise be repeated.

Q: "Why is separation speed critical in the callback?"
A: It runs inside the solver at every incumbent. A slow routine is paid
   many times over. In constraint generation it is paid once per round.

2. TOTAL UNIMODULARITY
================================================================

THE ONE-SENTENCE ANSWER
A matrix is totally unimodular when every square submatrix has
determinant 0, +1 or -1; for an integral right-hand side that forces
every vertex of the polyhedron to be integral, so the LP relaxation
returns the integer optimum without branching.

THE CHAIN, IN ORDER
  Cramer's rule: a vertex solves Bx = d for some square nonsingular
  submatrix B, so its coordinates are ratios of determinants with
  det(B) in the denominator. det(B) = +/-1 and d integral => integer
  vertex.
  Hoffman and Kruskal (1956) sharpen this to an if-and-only-if: for
  integral A, {x : Ax <= b, x >= 0} has integral vertices for EVERY
  integral b exactly when A is TU.

THE PROOF THEY WILL ASK FOR
Induction on the order k of a square submatrix B of the node-arc
incidence matrix. Each column of the full matrix has exactly one +1
(tail) and one -1 (head).
  k = 1: the entry is 0 or +/-1 by construction.
  k > 1, three cases:
    (a) some column of B is all zero        -> det(B) = 0
    (b) some column has one nonzero entry   -> expand along it, get
        +/-1 times an order k-1 determinant, which is in {0,+/-1} by
        the induction hypothesis
    (c) every column of B has both a +1 and a -1 -> every column sums
        to zero -> the rows sum to the zero vector -> B is singular
        -> det(B) = 0

WHY IT APPLIES HERE
Shortest path constraints are flow conservation (that incidence matrix)
plus bounds 0 <= x <= 1. Appending an identity block preserves TU. The
RHS is +1 at s, -1 at t, 0 elsewhere, all integral. So every vertex is
integral, and since a bounded LP attains its optimum at a vertex, the
simplex method returns a binary solution unasked. Matches what we
measured: zero gap on all ten, no fractional value, at most one node.

THE TRAP
Q: "Then why doesn't it work for longest path?"
A: Two separate reasons. The subtour constraints are not part of any TU
   system. And the odd-cycle incidence matrix is the standard
   counterexample: a triangle gives determinant 2. TU is fragile;
   adding constraints to a TU system usually destroys it.

Q: "Is TU necessary for integrality?"
A: Not for a single b. Hoffman-Kruskal's necessity is over ALL integral
   b. A specific instance can have integral vertices by luck.

3. ROBUST VERSUS STOCHASTIC, AND THE TIMING ASSUMPTION
================================================================

THE ONE-SENTENCE ANSWER
Robust optimises the worst realization over an uncertainty set;
stochastic optimises the expectation over a distribution. Neither means
anything until you say WHEN each decision is made relative to when the
uncertainty is observed.

THE ASSUMPTION, STATED
Factory-to-DC shipments x are committed BEFORE (c,u) is revealed.
DC-to-store deliveries y and unmet demand v are decided AFTER.
Justification: long-haul inbound is dispatched on a longer cycle than
local delivery, so by the time today's realized capacity is known the
inbound load is already in transit.

WHY THE ASSUMPTION IS LOAD-BEARING
  - It is why the first-stage capacity bound is x <= 0.99u, a worst-case
    bound sitting inside an average-case model: x is fixed blind, so it
    must fit every scenario.
  - Reverse the split, or defer everything until after the draw, and the
    model decomposes into independent deterministic problems. The
    stochastic formulation would then carry no information the nominal
    one does not.

THE THREE NUMBERS AND WHY THEY ORDER THAT WAY
  deterministic $415.00  <  stochastic $416.44  <  robust $421.57
Average-case protection costs less than worst-case protection and more
than assuming nothing goes wrong. If your numbers ever violate that
ordering, you have a bug.

THE POINT MOST LIKELY TO BE PROBED
Q: "Your costs are random. Why doesn't that change the answer?"
A: It doesn't, and that is provable, not empirical. E[c] = 0.5(0.99c) +
   0.5(1.01c) = c exactly, and expectation is linear, so the mean-cost
   problem IS the nominal problem. Only the CAPACITY randomness matters,
   and only because x is committed before capacity is seen. A symmetric
   mean-zero perturbation of objective coefficients alone never changes
   a risk-neutral stochastic linear program.

Q: "Why did the robust plan change the routing and not just the price?"
A: Because the change came from the capacity side, not the cost side. A
   uniform 1.01 scaling of every cost cannot reorder which route is
   cheapest. But shrinking every capacity by 1% strips 0.4 units off
   (d2,s1), which was sitting at its limit, and that has to be picked
   up elsewhere. It opens arc (d1,s1). That is also why the premium is
   1.58% rather than 1%: the excess is the cost of the forced reroute.

Q: "Justify the $50 penalty."
A: It has to exceed the dearest delivered unit, else the model prefers
   paying it to shipping. Dearest route is f1->d2->s2 at 4+3 = $7. So
   any p > 7 works. Above that threshold the answer is identical
   ($416.4624 at p = 8, 20, 50 and 500) because expected unmet demand is
   zero and the penalty is never paid. Below it, it bites: p = 5 abandons
   10.6 units, p = 1 ships nothing.

4. THE SUDOKU UNIQUENESS CUT
================================================================

THE ONE-SENTENCE ANSWER
Solving once gives A solution; adding a no-good cut that forbids exactly
that assignment and re-solving to infeasibility proves it is THE
solution.

THE CUT
  sum over all 81 cells of x[r, c, v*(r,c)]  <=  80
where v*(r,c) is the digit found in the first solve.

WHY IT IS CORRECT
The left side counts how many cells agree with that particular
assignment. It equals 81 only for that exact solution. Setting it <= 80
forbids that one point of the feasible set and nothing else: any other
valid completion differs in at least one cell, so its count is at most
80 and it survives. Infeasible on re-solve therefore means no other
completion exists.

THE GENERALIZATION THEY MAY ASK FOR
Repeat it k times, adding one cut per solution found, and you enumerate
the first k distinct solutions of any binary program. This is the
standard no-good / solution-elimination cut.

THE TRAP
Q: "22 clues is above the minimum of 17, so isn't it unique anyway?"
A: No. McGuire, Tugemann and Civario (2012) proved 17 is the minimum
   clue count for uniqueness to be POSSIBLE. It says nothing about any
   particular instance. Plenty of 30-clue puzzles have multiple
   solutions. Clue count is necessary, not sufficient, which is exactly
   why the cut is worth running.

Q: "Why equalities rather than <= 1 in the model?"
A: Nine cells and nine digits per row means "at most once" and "exactly
   once" coincide by counting. Equalities give the solver tighter
   information for free.

5. THE ONE THING TO VOLUNTEER
================================================================

If asked what you would change: the in-degree constraint. The first
version of the longest-path model had only an out-degree limit. Flow
conservation makes in-degree redundant at every node except t, where
b_t = -1 gives in(t) = out(t) + 1 and the out-degree limit only bounds
that by in(t) <= 2. So t could be entered twice and lie on a cycle, and
the separation routine generated 498 cuts over node sets containing t
across the ten instances -- cuts the written family, indexed over
S subset V\{s,t}, never authorized. Adding the in-degree family forces
out(t) = 0, confines every cycle to V\{s,t}, and makes the written model
and the executed model the same object. It also cut the total round
count from 739 to 255.

Knowing where your own model was wrong, and why, is worth more in a viva
than any of the numbers.
